# Come and See: project instructions

## Working location

This repository checkout is the foundation for the owner's existing and future
Come and See work. Edit and review files here before committing them to GitHub.
The remote repository is <https://github.com/MoneyforNotn/Come-and-See>.

Use this checkout and its existing Git history. Do not initialize a replacement
repository or maintain a separate copy as the source of published content.
After completing each round of repository work, summarize the pending changes
and ask the owner whether to commit them. Once approved, commit all pending
public repository changes, including previously staged work covered by the
approval. Ask about pushing to GitHub in the same review and push when approved.
Keep private source material and credentials out of the commit.

## Project purpose

Build a growing, clear, source-supported collection of learning notes,
interesting ideas, research, book notes, and explanations that can be shared
with others. Future learning guides, routines, and educational videos may draw
on this collection.

## Public library and private notes

The owner has chosen a public reference library with private raw notes kept
outside this repository checkout. Keep raw learning notes, transcripts, and
personal source material outside the checkout; do not stage or commit them.
Only bring material intended for public readers into the repository, with
private details removed and source attribution preserved where appropriate.

A folder inside this checkout is not private merely because of its name or an
ignore rule. No private notes storage location has been designated yet.

## Preserve and inspect

Preserve existing writing and its provenance during reorganization. Track
renames, keep historical material recoverable, and distinguish unfinished
placeholders from completed articles. Use Windows-compatible filenames.

Imported content has not been verified merely because it is in this repository.
Distinguish sourced findings, interpretations, hypotheses, and personal beliefs.
Investigate unconventional claims on their evidence. Never invent citations,
quotations, or claims of having read unavailable source material.

## Agreed evidence categories

Classify developed entries by the categories they contain. Identify the category
at the claim or section level when an article combines different kinds of claims:

1. Documented events and findings.
2. Interpretations drawn from evidence.
3. Hypotheses and disputed claims.
4. Personal experiences, beliefs, and philosophical positions.
5. Questions for which the evidence is insufficient.

These are kinds of claims, not numerical confidence scores. A documented
statement establishes what its speaker said, not necessarily its truth.
Keep editorial status (Draft, Reviewed, Needs update) separate from evidence
category. Do not label legacy articles Reviewed without checking them.

## Research workflow

Use Capture -> Research -> Draft -> Review -> Publish. Define a bounded
question, inspect the supplied material, trace important claims to original
sources, seek counterevidence, and distinguish the source's argument from the
repository's assessment. Put citations beside the claims they support. Record
source coverage, dates, uncertainty, and meaningful corrections.

Write articles as explanations of their topics, rather than accounts of an
interview or discussion. Avoid framing such as "both speakers agree" or organizing
the main article by interviews. Agreement in a conversation is not evidence that
a claim is true. Keep sources traceable through nearby citations and a source
register. Attribute consequential allegations and distinctive speculative models
where their origin matters; do not turn them into unattributed facts.

The opening "In brief" must stand on its own: include the central context,
mechanisms, memorable insights, useful takeaways, major disagreements, and the
most consequential speculations. Clearly identify what is documented,
interpretive, speculative, disputed, or unresolved. Do not leave a central idea
only in the body or an appendix, and do not replace the ideas with a verdict.
Preserve conditional language and distinguish questions from assertions.

Keep supplementary research tied to the source's important claims. Put detailed
background in an appendix when it would displace the main account. Separate
source summary, connections drawn by the repository, and evidence assessment.
Two interviews with the same speaker are not independent corroboration. The
article template is a useful default, not a compulsory format for every note,
book entry, timeline, or other document. Omit sections that add no value.

Keep unconventional and philosophical questions open where evidence cannot
settle them. Distinguish unverified, disputed, contradicted, and untestable claims;
neither lack of disproof nor familiarity establishes truth. Review source fidelity
and evidence quality separately. See [the editorial workflow](docs/editorial-workflow.md).

Keep raw transcripts outside the checkout. Disclose auto-generated captions and
any unverified audio or visual details. A transcript is a source for what a
speaker argued, not independent validation of that argument.

The detailed subject-folder migration remains a proposal. Do not move unrelated
collections merely because their future taxonomy has been discussed.

Use the shared topic headers in `assets/headers/` for articles and README files;
follow that folder's visual guidance. Keep raw sources outside the checkout.
The project chat is the editorial intake: persist accepted decisions here or in
project documentation so future work does not depend on remembering a chat.
